The email is four lines long, and it apologizes three times.

"Sorry to bother you! I've attached the draft. I hope that's okay. Sorry if it's not what you wanted, I can redo any of it. Does that make sense?"

The draft, when we open it, is excellent.

So what's all the apologizing for? Tom, who wrote it, isn't really asking about the draft. He's asking about himself.

## What approval wants

Tom is showing the **approval** need: to be praised, validated, liked, and above all seen as *good*.

It's a close cousin of acceptance, only hungrier for compliments. The clearest way to hold the difference is this:

> Acceptance says, "I want to belong." Approval says, "I need permission."

The hidden fear is disapproval. Being seen as bad. Letting someone down. People with this need usually avoid conflict, and they'll stretch themselves thin to keep everyone's good opinion.

## What it sounds like

Approval softens everything. The tone hedges, cushions, and checks.

<Phrases>
- "People say I'm easy to work with."
- "I'm just trying to help."
- "I probably shouldn't say this, but..."
- "I'm probably rambling."
- "Does that sound okay?"
- "I hope that makes sense."
</Phrases>

Each of those is a small request for a ruling. *Tell me I did all right.*

## What it can look like

Visually, we might notice someone slightly overdressed for the occasion: crisp, safe, with one small touch that seems to say "please like me."

In conversation there's often a lot of *over*. Over-nodding. Over-complimenting. Over-apologizing. Laughing at a joke that wasn't very funny. There's self-deprecating humor that fishes for reassurance, and the neat trick of waving away a compliment while clearly hoping for another.

These are hints, not proof. Some people are simply polite. Speech is the stronger evidence, so we wait for the hedges before we decide.

## Language that lands

General praise slides right off an approval person. "Great job!" is too vague to trust.

What lands is **specific praise for a specific behavior**.

- "The way you handled that client call was exactly right."
- "You caught the error on page three. Nobody else did."
- "I trust your judgment completely on this."

Specifics work because they're believable. They prove we were actually paying attention, and they answer the real question: yes, you did it well, and here's the evidence.

<Callout label="Can't tell which cousin?">
If we can't work out whether we're hearing acceptance or approval, we treat it as both. "We're lucky to have you, and that report was spot on" covers the group and the verdict in one sentence.
</Callout>

## Acceptance or approval? A quick test

Try these two.

"I hate walking into a party where I don't know anyone." There's a group, and the worry is being outside it. That's **acceptance**.

"I'd hate for the host to think I was rude for leaving early." There's a judge, and the worry is the ruling. That's **approval**.

Ask what the person is afraid of losing. A place, or a good opinion?

## The superpower

People with the approval need spend their lives studying how others feel, and it makes them remarkable at it.

Their gift is **deep empathy**. They notice the colleague who went quiet, the friend who's pretending to be fine. On a team, they're often the first to know that something's wrong.
