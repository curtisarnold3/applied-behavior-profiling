A lighthouse does one thing. It stands on a rock and sends out a beam, and ships twenty miles off change course because of it.

Nobody on those ships has met the keeper. They've never seen the lamp room, or the stairs, or the weekly routine of trimming and polishing that keeps the light burning.

They just see the beam, and they trust it.

Authority works in much the same way. What people respond to is the beam. What produces it is somewhere they can't see.

## Three corners

The Authority Triangle is a one-page summary of the self-assessment. It has three parts.

<AuthorityTriangle />

**Internal: behavior.** These are the five traits: confidence, discipline, leadership, gratitude, enjoyment. They live inside us and show up in how we act.

**External: effect.** This is how other people respond to us. Whether they listen, follow, trust, relax.

**Underlying: habits.** These are the five areas: environment, time, appearance, social, financial. They sit beneath everything else.

## How the corners connect

The connections matter more than the corners.

**Internal drives external.** People can't see our confidence or our discipline directly. They see what those traits *do*: the steady voice, the kept promise, the credit handed to someone else. The effect on them follows from that.

That's worth sitting with. We can't work on the external corner directly. We can't make people trust us. We can only change what's producing the response.

**Habits hold up both.** The traits rest on the habits. It's hard to feel confident walking out of a chaotic home, late, in clothes we grabbed off the floor. It's hard to be generous when money is a constant alarm.

The lighthouse keeper's weekly routine isn't glamorous. It's also the only reason there's a light.

## Reading the triangle backward

The most practical use of this model is as a diagnostic, and it runs in reverse.

Start with an **effect** we don't like. People talk over us in meetings. Our suggestions get ignored. Friends don't quite rely on us.

Then ask which **trait** would produce that effect. Being talked over often traces to confidence. Not being relied on often traces to discipline.

Then ask which **habit** is underneath. Unreliable follow-through might sit on a time habit: no system for tracking what we've promised.

Now we have something we can change on a Tuesday. We can't fix "people don't rely on me." We can start writing down every promise we make.

## Where our scores go

Take the results from the self-assessment and place them:

- The five trait scores go on the **internal** corner.
- The five habit scores go on the **underlying** corner.
- The **external** corner gets no score from us. It's what we'll watch over the coming weeks to see whether the work is paying off.

Then circle two things: the lowest trait and the lowest habit area. Remember the rule from the last lesson. Fixing a weakness beats building a strength, and the lowest trait has the biggest effect on influence.

## A caution about the external corner

It's tempting to skip to the outside and work on *seeming* authoritative. A deeper voice. A firmer handshake. A power pose in the bathroom mirror.

That's painting the beam onto the lighthouse. From a distance, in daylight, it might pass. At night in a storm, which is when authority gets tested, there's nothing there.

The triangle is a reminder of the order things go in. Habits, then traits, then effect.

<Callout label="The model in one line">
Habits build traits. Traits create the effect. We can only work on the first two.
</Callout>

## Why this belongs in a profiling course

It might seem we've changed subjects. We haven't.

For seven weeks we've learned to say the right thing to the right person. But the same sentence carries different weight from different mouths. When someone with real authority says "I believe you," it lands. When someone we don't trust says it, it sounds like a technique.

So this corner of the course isn't separate from the rest. It's what gives the rest its force.
