A seven-year-old sits down at a new lunch table. Nobody looks up.

He tries a joke. One kid laughs. He tries another, bigger, and now three of them are laughing and somebody slides over to make room.

He has just learned something that will outlast everything else he learns that year: *being funny gets me a seat.*

Thirty years on, he's the one cracking jokes in the tense budget meeting. His colleagues think it's his personality. It's closer to a strategy, and he worked it out before he could ride a bike.

## Three things every child is solving for

Children build their behavior around three needs.

<ChildhoodTriangle />

**Friends.** What did I have to do to earn and keep friends? To belong, to feel loved?

**Safety.** What did I have to do to feel safe, emotionally and physically?

**Rewards.** What did I have to do to get praise, attention, and validation from adults and other kids?

When all three come easily, a child doesn't have to think much about them. When one runs short, the child adapts. The adaptation works, so it sticks. And then it grows up.

## What each shortfall becomes

| What ran short | What it tends to become |
| --- | --- |
| Friends | Chameleons, people pleasers, overachievers |
| Safety | Hyper-controllers, avoiders, chronic apologizers |
| Rewards | Performers, approval seekers, status chasers |

Our lunch-table comedian had a friends shortfall. So did the colleague who agrees with whoever spoke last.

## Four people we already know

Let's make this concrete. We've all met these four.

**The one who can't take a compliment.** We say "great job" and they wave it off, change the subject, or explain why it wasn't really that good. Growing up, praise was rare, or it came with criticism right behind it. A compliment still feels like the first half of a sentence.

**The one who avoids all conflict.** They'll agree to almost anything to keep the peace. In their house, disagreement meant screaming, or a parent who went silent for days. Conflict didn't mean a difference of opinion. It meant danger.

**The constant name-dropper.** Every story features someone important. This is someone who felt invisible, and learned that standing near impressive people was a way to be seen.

**The one who panics when plans change.** A moved meeting or a late guest and they're visibly rattled. Home was unpredictable, so they learned that control equals safety. A changed plan isn't an inconvenience. It's the ground shifting.

Notice what happened as we read those. Each person got a little less annoying and a little more understandable.

## Patterned, not personal

That's the key line of this whole unit: **behavior is patterned, not personal.**

When someone is difficult with us, it feels aimed at us. It almost never is. The pattern was set long before we walked in, and we just happened to be standing where it fires.

So we're not looking at a difficult adult. We're looking at a child's survival strategy wearing a grown-up face.

That's not an excuse for bad behavior. It's an explanation, and explanations are what let us respond instead of react.

## It works on us, too

There's a reason this lesson asks us to match *other* people to the triangle first. It's easier.

But the same three questions apply to us. What did we have to do for friends, for safety, for praise? Which of those is still quietly running our Tuesday afternoons?

Self-understanding is the gateway to self-mastery, and self-mastery is one whole side of the Success Triangle. We'll come back to it in Week 8.
