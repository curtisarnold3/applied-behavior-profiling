Three weeks into learning this, a student named Paulo profiled his brother-in-law.

"Strength," he said, with total confidence. "Classic. Tough, never asks for help, always says he's got it handled."

Then he tried the strength playbook at a family dinner: respect, directness, asking his advice. It went nowhere. The brother-in-law got quieter.

What Paulo had missed was a line the man used constantly: "It's handled. That okay with everyone?" He wasn't asking for respect. He was asking whether he'd done well.

It looked like strength. It was approval, wearing strength's jacket.

## Why misreads happen

Behavior on the surface can match one category while the reason underneath belongs to another. Two people can do the same thing for opposite reasons.

That's why the first rule matters so much: listen for why they felt the need to say it. The *why* is what we're profiling.

Here are the four mistakes that catch nearly everyone.

## Mistake 1: Confusing acceptance with approval

They're cousins, and they show up together often.

**Acceptance** is about the group: *am I in?* **Approval** is about the verdict: *am I good?*

"I didn't want to be the only one who skipped it" is acceptance. "I didn't want them to think badly of me" is approval.

And if we truly can't tell, we speak to both.

## Mistake 2: Reading a permanent type instead of the moment

"She's a significance person." We've all said some version of it.

It's the easiest trap in the course, because types are tidy and moments aren't. But the same person shows different needs in different circles, and can decide differently at work and at home.

A profile is a reading of what's showing *here, now, with us*. It's a weather report. It isn't a birth certificate.

## Mistake 3: Speaking from our own need

We give what we'd like to get.

Someone who wants to feel smart tells everyone how clever they are. Someone who wants to belong tells the loner how well she fits in. It's well meant, and it misses.

A useful check before we speak: *is this what they've been reaching for, or what I would want to hear?*

## Mistake 4: Confusing similar categories

Some needs, pillars, and values sound alike. A few even share a name's worth of meaning across layers. Here's a field guide to the pairs that trip people most.

| Looks like | Might really be | The tell |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strength | Approval | Toughness followed by a check: "that okay?" Strength doesn't ask |
| Detachment ("I don't need people") | Acceptance | A hidden fear of rejection. Leaving first is safer than being left out |
| Significance | Recognition (value) | Rank versus worth. "Be the best" versus "know it mattered" |
| Intelligence | Information (value) | An audience versus none. Corrects us versus asks another question |
| Deviance | Novelty | Different from the crowd versus new to me |
| Social | Conformity | Named people versus general norms |
| Recognition | Growth | Impact already made versus trajectory ahead |
| Acceptance (need) | Connection (value) | Included in this group versus truly known |

The pattern across the whole table is the same. When two things look alike, we ask what the person is **afraid of losing**, or what they're **ultimately after**. The answer splits them.

## How to correct in the moment

So what should Paulo have done at that dinner?

**Notice the miss.** The clearest sign of a wrong read is that matching language doesn't land. No softening, no lift. Sometimes a slight pulling back.

**Don't push harder.** The instinct is to repeat the same thing louder. That only confirms we're not listening.

**Go back to the words.** What did they actually say? What keeps repeating? Paulo already had "that okay with everyone?" in his ears. He just hadn't weighed it.

**Try the neighbor.** Misreads usually land one category over. If strength didn't work, approval is a sensible next guess: "You handled that really well tonight."

Then watch again.

## Keep the alternative

There's a habit that prevents most of these errors, and it's built into this week's deliverable.

For every profile, write down **one alternative read** and why we ruled it out. "I considered approval, but there was no checking for a verdict."

It takes ten seconds. It forces us to look at the evidence once more, and when our first read is wrong, the alternative is often the right one, already sitting on the page.

<Callout label="Field rule">
A read is a hypothesis. If the matching language doesn't land, we've learned something, and we try the neighbor.
</Callout>
