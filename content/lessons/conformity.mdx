Walter backs out of the parking space and pulls in again.

He was over the line. Only by a few inches, and the lot's half empty, but the lines are there for a reason. On the second try he's dead center, and he gets out of the car with the small, private satisfaction of a thing done properly.

Nobody saw. That's not the point. Walter would know.

## How conformity decides

Walter uses the **conformity** pillar. He makes choices according to rules, structure, the proper way, and what other people do. That last part means the wider culture, not just the people he knows.

What he's after is predictability and the sense of doing what's right. It's the mirror image of deviance. Where Jess from the deviance lesson hears "that's how it's done" as a reason to do the opposite, Walter hears it as a reason to proceed.

## What it sounds like

<Phrases>
- "Those are really popular right now."
- "I just want to do the right thing."
- "What's the proper way?"
- "We've always done it this way."
- "That's just how it's done."
- "Technically we're supposed to..."
</Phrases>

The tone is structured and polite. Conformity cites its sources: authority, policy, tradition. When Walter justifies a choice, there's usually a rule or a precedent standing behind him.

## What it can look like

We might see upright, symmetrical posture, hands kept close, and clothing that matches the group's norms: modest, pressed, appropriate. Posted rules get followed.

Remember these are generalizations, and weaker than words. Neat people aren't all rule-followers. We listen for the appeal to how things are *supposed* to be.

## How to frame it

We frame the idea as **standard, proven, best practice, respectable**.

- "This is the gold standard in your field."
- "It's the approach most firms like yours have adopted."
- "There's a clear process, and here are the steps."

Walter wants to know that sensible people have gone this way before him and that there's a railing to hold.

**What to avoid:** disruptive or anti-establishment language, and change without structure. "Let's tear it up and see what happens" is, to a conformity decider, a description of a disaster.

## An example

We want Walter to switch the office to a new filing system.

The novelty pitch, "it's brand new, nobody around here has it yet," makes him nervous. Untested is not a selling point.

The conformity pitch: "It's what the regulator recommends now, and the two biggest firms in town moved over last year. There's a standard migration checklist, and I've printed it."

A checklist. He's in.

## Conformity or social?

We met this pair in the last lesson. From Walter's side, the test is the same: names or norms?

**Social** protects specific people in a small circle. **Conformity** follows the larger culture and its rules.

Walter might well follow a rule even when it annoys a friend. Maria, from the social lesson, would bend the rule for the friend.

## The red line to deviance

Now for the thing we promised in the deviance lesson.

There's a line running between conformity and deviance. Very high conformity often comes with a proportionally strong streak of **private deviance**. The tighter the public rule-following, the more pressure builds, and pressure looks for a release.

Think of a pressure cooker. The lid's on tight, and that's exactly why there's a valve.

This is useful to understand. It explains why the most buttoned-up person in the office sometimes surprises everyone.

<Callout label="Know it. Never raise it.">
This is knowledge for our own understanding only. We don't hint at it, joke about it, or go looking for it. Pointing at someone's private side breaks trust instantly, and it's exactly the kind of judgment that ends our ability to help.
</Callout>
