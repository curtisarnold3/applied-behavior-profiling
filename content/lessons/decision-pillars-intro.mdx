Six friends are trying to pick a restaurant for Saturday night.

One wants the place with no sign on the door that nobody's supposed to know about. One wants the Ethiopian spot that opened Tuesday. One keeps asking whether everybody's okay with spicy. One suggests the steakhouse, because that's where people go for a birthday. One says they've been going to Luigi's for eleven years and sees no reason to stop now. And one finally says, "Okay, but what are we doing? I need to eat by seven."

Here's what's interesting. All six want the same thing from the evening: a good night with people they like. They agree on the *why*. They can't agree on dinner because each of them **decides** differently.

## Fuel and GPS

In Unit 2 we learned to hear what people are chasing from others. That was the need.

A need is the fuel. It tells us what someone is emotionally after. It doesn't tell us which road they'll take.

That's the job of a **decision pillar**: the filter a person uses to choose their next move and to justify it afterward. If the need is the fuel, the pillar is the GPS.

Two people with the very same need can decide in completely different ways. Two people who both want significance might choose opposite cars, one because nobody else has it and one because it's what successful people drive.

## Why this matters

Here's the uncomfortable part. We can read someone's need perfectly, speak to it warmly, and still get a no.

That happens when we frame the offer through the wrong pillar. The person feels understood and then hears a proposal that sounds wrong to them, and what we get back is resistance, confusion, or a polite rejection. The idea was fine. The framing wasn't theirs.

Reading pillars lets us do three things:

- **Tailor the framing**, so a good idea sounds like a good idea to this particular person.
- **Predict objections** before they're spoken.
- **Guide decisions calmly**, without pushing.

## The six pillars

Let's go back to our six friends, because each one is a pillar.

| Friend | Pillar | They decide by... |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The rule-breaker | Deviance | Doing the opposite of what's expected |
| The one always trying something new | Novelty | What's new, exciting, different |
| The heart of the group | Social | How it affects the people close to them |
| The rule-follower | Conformity | Structure, rules, what others do |
| The loyalist who won't quit a bad show | Investment | What they've already put in |
| The "okay, but what are we doing?" fixer | Necessity | What has to happen now |

Each pillar gets its own short lesson this week.

## Where to listen

With needs, we listened for what people kept reaching for. Pillars show up somewhere slightly different: in **justifications**.

Whenever someone explains or defends a choice, especially one they make over and over, we're hearing their decision filter at work. "We've always done it this way." "I've already put so much into it." "Why would I do what everyone else does?"

People rarely announce their pillar. They announce their reasons, and the reasons give it away.

## The same ground rules

Everything from Unit 2 still holds.

**Read the moment.** Someone can decide by conformity at the office and by novelty on vacation.

**Two is fine.** If we hear two pillars, we frame for both.

**Words first.** We'll mention a few things we might see with each pillar. They're weak, general hints. What people say when they justify a choice is far stronger evidence.

A rejected proposal isn't always a bad proposal. Often it's a good one that arrived in the wrong frame.
