The waiter hands out menus, and Jess doesn't open hers.

"Can the kitchen do the short rib, but over the polenta from the lamb dish? And no sauce." It's not on the menu. It's never on the menu. Jess has been ordering off-menu since she was nineteen, and she parked in the loading zone to get here.

Is she being difficult? From the inside, no. She's being herself, and for her that means *not what everyone else is having*.

## How deviance decides

Jess uses the **deviance** pillar. She makes choices by breaking patterns and norms, standing apart, being bold or disruptive.

There's an idea underneath it: transformation through contrast. Things get better, in this view, when somebody refuses to do them the normal way.

The detail that matters most is **pride**. A deviance decider doesn't break the pattern by accident, or apologize for it. They're pleased about it.

## What it sounds like

<Phrases>
- "They said I couldn't, so I did."
- "That's just the system talking."
- "I don't care what everyone else is doing."
- "I've never liked doing things the traditional way."
- "Why do we even do it this way?"
</Phrases>

The tone is blunt, often sarcastic, and built on contrast: *them* over there, *me* over here. Deviance deciders love to play devil's advocate. If the room agrees, they'll find the other side just to see what happens.

## What it can look like

A few visual hints: unconventional style, tattoos, bold choices of one kind or another. A relaxed, leaning-back posture, as if the rules of the meeting don't quite apply. And small acts like the off-menu order or the creative parking spot.

Hold these loosely. Plenty of people with tattoos follow every rule there is. Appearance is a general cue and a weak one. The justification is what tells us.

## How to frame it

To a deviance decider, the most off-putting phrase in the language is "everyone's doing it." That's not a reason. That's a warning.

So we frame the idea as **breaking the pattern** and challenging the status quo.

- "This isn't the usual route, but it works."
- "Most people don't have the nerve to try this. I figured you're not most people."
- "The standard approach is what everybody else will do. Here's the other way."

**What to avoid:** groupthink, tradition, and any version of "this is what most people choose." Each of those pushes them toward the exit.

## An example

Suppose we want Jess to adopt a new scheduling tool at work.

The conformity pitch would be: "It's the industry standard, and most teams our size already use it." For Jess, that's two reasons to say no.

The deviance pitch: "Honestly, most teams run this the default way and get default results. I think we could set it up differently from anyone else here."

Same tool. Now it's hers.

## Deviance or novelty?

These two are the most commonly confused pair in this unit, and the next lesson is novelty, so let's draw the line now.

**Deviance** wants *different from the crowd*. The reference point is other people.

**Novelty** wants *new*. The reference point is time.

Jess would happily order the same strange off-menu dish for ten years, as long as nobody else is ordering it. A novelty decider would be bored of it by the second visit.

<Callout label="A fact to keep to ourselves">
Deviance has a hidden relationship with its opposite, conformity. We'll get to it in the conformity lesson. It's the kind of thing we know and never say out loud.
</Callout>
