Ask Leo about his year and we don't get a summary. We get scenes.

The night bus over a mountain pass with the door tied shut with rope. The street in Lisbon where an old man taught him a card game. The job he quit in April ("it was fine, it was just *fine*") and the three weeks he spent learning to weld.

He doesn't own much. His savings are modest. And he has more stories than anyone we know.

## The end goal

Leo's value is **experiences**: a life that's *felt*. Richness, story, intensity.

He's told us so himself, more or less: he isn't trying to have a perfect life. He's trying to have a full one.

## Where it often comes from

This value often grows from **dull, rigid environments** where spontaneity was shut down.

A childhood of beige routine. A house where "because that's how we do it" ended every conversation and nothing unplanned was allowed to happen. The child from that house grows up determined that their one life won't be spent the same way.

## How it shows up

- A hunger for variety: travel, new places, new food.
- Frequent changes of job, hobby, even style.
- Always proposing something: a trip, a detour, a thing to try.
- A dread of routine.

And underneath all of it, one fear. For an experiences person, the worst outcome isn't failure. It's feeling **numb**.

## What to listen for

<Phrases>
- "I don't want a perfect life. I want a full one."
- "Ultimately, I just want to feel like I really lived."
- "It was fine. It was just... fine."
</Phrases>

That third one is easy to miss. For most people, "fine" is acceptable. For Leo it's a complaint.

In the adjectives: they admire people who are *alive*, *spontaneous*, *fearless*, *up for anything*.

## How to move them

We offer **feelings and memories**, not facts.

The instruction is to help them imagine **the moment, not the outcome**.

Suppose we're inviting Leo to join a project. The factual pitch goes: "It's a twelve-month contract with good pay and clear deliverables." All true. He's already looking out the window.

The experiences pitch: "Picture the first morning on site. Nobody's built one of these here before. In a year you'll be telling people about it."

We've given him a scene he can step into. That's what he decides with.

## What to avoid

- Leading with security, stability, or predictability. To Leo those are costs.
- Spreadsheets and bullet points as the opening move.
- Describing anything as "the sensible choice."

## Experiences or novelty?

These two sit very close, so let's separate them.

**Novelty** is a **decision pillar**. It's how someone *chooses*: given two options, take the new one.

**Experiences** is a **value**. It's what someone is *ultimately after*: a life rich in feeling.

They often travel together, and when they do, we frame for both. But they can come apart. Someone can value experiences and decide by investment, returning to the same mountain village every summer for twenty years because of everything that place holds for them. The value is the full life. The pillar is just how they pick the next step toward it.

## A thought for the rest of us

Experiences people can look reckless from a steadier chair. Quitting a fine job? Spending the savings on a trip?

It's worth sitting with their question for a minute, though. When we look back, what will we actually remember: the fine years, or the night bus with the door tied shut?
