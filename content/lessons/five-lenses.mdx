A gray sedan swerves into our lane with about a foot to spare, and we slam the brakes.

What happens in the next two seconds? For most of us, a verdict. *Idiot. Maniac. People like that shouldn't have a license.*

Now suppose we learn the driver's daughter is in the back seat having an asthma attack, and the hospital is the next exit. Nothing about the swerve changed. Everything about how we see the driver did.

That shift is what this lesson is about. Before we can read anyone, we have to notice the lens we're reading through.

## Five stages, not five options

The course calls them lenses, but they behave more like stages of development. We climb them.

<StagesLadder />

**Stage 1: Separation and reactivity.** We see other people as **broken**. Ego runs the show. There's constant judging and comparing, and the options are defend or attack. The world feels hostile, and there's very little self-awareness.

**Stage 2: Comparison and judgment.** We see others as **different**. We're starting to notice nuance, but the differences still irritate us. Curiosity has arrived. Ego is still filtering it.

**Stage 3: Observation and detachment.** We see others as **facts**. We step back and react less. A person's situation becomes pattern and history, not something aimed at us.

**Stage 4: Empathy and understanding.** We see others as **reasons**. Every behavior, even a harmful one, has a reason behind it: pain, conditioning, history. Compassion stops being a technique and shows up on its own.

**Stage 5: Unity and reflection.** We see others as **reflections**. What we notice in them mirrors our own inner world. Judgment is mostly gone, and every interaction teaches us something about ourselves.

Here's a handy way to tell the top two apart. Stage 4 is about *them*. Stage 5 is about *us*.

## Life as an inkblot test

Why would two people see the same swerve so differently?

Because perception isn't a camera. It's a prediction the brain builds from everything that's happened to us before. The neuroscientist Anil Seth calls it a "controlled hallucination."

So every event works a bit like an inkblot. Two people look at the same shape and describe different pictures, because each brings a different past to it. What we watch and read all day shapes the picture too.

This gives us our first real profiling tool. When someone treats a small inconvenience like a crisis, their reaction tells us more about what's going on inside them than about the event. Often it's control, anxiety, old trauma, or insecurity.

The reaction is the data.

## Perspective can be trained

None of this is fixed. Under stress, we can zoom out, the way a camera pulls back from one car to show the whole highway.

At first that's a technique we have to remember. The goal is for the higher stage to become who we are, so there's nothing to remember.

## The six wound types

If behavior has reasons, what are they? Very often, an old wound. There are six.

<WoundTypes />

| Wound | How it forms | Adult signs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Social | Excluded, rejected, bullied, always the new kid | Echoes others' opinions, agrees before we finish, avoids conflict, over-apologizes, fishes for reassurance |
| Authority | Micromanaged, or left with no structure at all | Reacts strongly to criticism from above, challenges rules, needs control, drifts into charge |
| Emotional | Let down repeatedly; a parent or friends who left | Guarded, dodges personal questions, keeps things shallow, jokes when it gets deep |
| Security | Unstable home, money trouble, volatile caregivers, frequent moves | Worst-case thinking, avoids risk, hyper-aware of small threats, stressed by a broken routine |
| Validation | Praised only for grades or performance | Brings up achievements unprompted, crushed by small criticism, uneasy out of the spotlight |
| Trust | Caregivers who lied or broke promises | Shares little, keeps people at arm's length, tests loyalty, "they have to earn my trust" |

Two of these get confused, so let's separate them now. The **emotional** wound says *don't hurt me*. The **trust** wound says *don't lie to me*. The trust wound is the hardest of the six to unwind.

One practical note on the security wound: it helps to let the person predict what's coming. Tell them the plan. Surprises are the enemy.

## More than one, so we triage

Most people carry more than one wound. That includes us.

So we think like an emergency room. We don't treat everything at once. We start with the wound driving the behavior we most want to change.

And the goal isn't to erase it. The goal is to turn a wound into a **scar**. A wound needs constant attention. A scar is just part of the story. We get there by checking in on it regularly, without beating ourselves up.
