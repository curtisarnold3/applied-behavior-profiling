Most of us have had this small shock. We run into our dentist at a concert.

In the chair she's brisk, exact, a little formal. Here she's in a band T-shirt with three friends from college, shouting the lyrics, and when she spots us she grabs our arm like we're old pals.

Which one is the real her?

Both. We've just seen her in two different circles.

## Four circles

Picture a person standing at the center of four rings, like the rings of a target. Each ring holds a different set of people.

<FourCircles />

**Circle 1: Inner.** Partners, mentors, very close family. This is where raw needs live. What someone shows here often matches the need they showed their parents.

**Circle 2: Legacy.** Long-time friends, the tribe. The people who knew us before. This ring is why someone can act one way out with friends and another way entirely at home.

**Circle 3: Proximity.** Coworkers and acquaintances. The relationship is good, but it isn't close. Our dentist, in the chair, has us here.

**Circle 4: Outer.** Strangers. This is about how a person wants to be seen by people they'll never speak to: on the street, in traffic, across a restaurant.

## One person, several needs

Here's the idea that clears up a lot of confusion.

The same person may show a different need in each circle. A man might want **strength** from strangers, **intelligence** from coworkers, **acceptance** from his oldest friends, and **approval** from his wife.

None of those is fake. Each ring taught him something different about what works.

Some people show the same need in every ring. That happens too. There's no rule that the circles have to differ.

## It's the playground again

We've met this idea before. In the first lesson of this unit, we remembered being one child in the classroom, another on the playground, and a third at home.

The circles are a more detailed version of the same thing. Different audiences, different strategies. We learned them early, and we never stopped.

It also explains something that puzzles a lot of new profilers. We read a coworker carefully for months, then meet their spouse, who describes someone we barely recognize. We weren't wrong. We were reading circle 3, and the spouse lives in circle 1.

## The part that saves us in the field

Now for the good news, because this could easily turn into a headache. Do we have to work out which circle we're in before we can say anything?

No.

<Callout label="Field rule">
The circle doesn't have to be known. Identify the need, test it, use it, move on.
</Callout>

The circles are an explanation, not a step in the process. They tell us why needs shift from place to place, so we aren't thrown when they do.

In a live conversation there's only one question that matters: what is this person showing right now, with me? Whatever circle we happen to be in, that's the need in front of us, and that's the one we answer.

## Identify, test, use, move on

Let's spell that four-word method out, since it's the working rhythm for everything from here on.

1. **Identify.** We hear a phrase and form a first guess. "I hope that's okay" sounds like approval.
2. **Test.** We offer a little matching language and watch. "You handled that really well." Did they soften? Light up? Shrug it off?
3. **Use.** If it landed, we keep speaking that language. If it didn't, we haven't lost anything. We've learned what it isn't.
4. **Move on.** We don't stop the conversation to admire our read. The point is the person, not the profile.

A miss costs almost nothing. A kind sentence that wasn't quite the right kind is still a kind sentence.
