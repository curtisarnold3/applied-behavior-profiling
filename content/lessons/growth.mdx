Ana's nightstand has a stack on it.

A book on negotiation, with a pencil in it. A Portuguese grammar. A notebook titled "Q3." Her phone holds a running app, a meditation app, and a course on statistics she's two-thirds through.

She has a good job, and last month she turned down a promotion. "It was the same work with a bigger title," she said. "I'd have stopped learning anything."

Most people would have taken the title. What is Ana optimizing for?

## The end goal

Ana's value is **growth**: to evolve, and to become more than she is now.

Not *more than other people*. More than *last year's Ana*. The comparison that matters to her is with her earlier self.

## Where it often comes from

Growth often comes out of **stagnant environments**, places where staying small was rewarded.

A town, a family, or a school where ambition was treated as showing off and "who do you think you are?" was the standard reply to a new idea. The child who chafed against that becomes the adult with the stack on the nightstand.

## How it shows up

- Constant learning: always a course, a book, a new practice.
- Goals, tracked. Often written down.
- A habit of optimizing routines, tools, and habits.
- A pull toward progress over comfort.

And one pattern that surprises the people around them: growth people **leave** jobs and relationships once the growth stops. It can look disloyal. From their side, staying somewhere they've stopped growing feels like a slow suffocation.

## What to listen for

<Phrases>
- "I don't need it easy. I need to know I'm not stuck."
- "I just want to become the best version of myself."
- "Ultimately, I just want to keep getting better."
</Phrases>

In the adjectives: they admire people who are *driven*, *disciplined*, *always improving*.

## How to move them

Here's the key instruction: speak to their **trajectory**, not their **status**.

Status is where someone is. Trajectory is where they're heading.

"You're one of our top performers" is a status statement. Ana hears it and feels oddly flat. It's a full stop.

"You're handling things now that you couldn't have touched a year ago, and this next project stretches you further" is a trajectory statement. It shows the progress and points at the next self. That's the sentence that moves her.

## The promotion, revisited

Think about what her manager actually offered: more title, same work. In growth terms, that was a status upgrade with a flat trajectory. Of course she said no.

Suppose the offer had been framed around what was true and growing in it: "You'd be managing people for the first time. It's a completely different skill, and frankly you'll be bad at it for six months." That might well have been a yes.

Notice that the second pitch promises difficulty. For most values that's a drawback. For growth, it's the attraction.

## What to avoid

- Selling comfort, ease, or "you've earned a rest."
- Praising where they are without pointing anywhere.
- Roles with no visible next step.

## Growth or recognition?

We met this pair in the recognition lesson. Here's the other side of it.

**Recognition** looks back: see what I did, and that it mattered. Speak to **impact**.

**Growth** looks forward: show me what I'm becoming. Speak to **trajectory**.

A quick test is the finished achievement. Give Imani, from the recognition lesson, a sincere account of what her work changed, and it means a great deal. Give Ana the same speech, and she'll thank us and ask what's next.

## Six values, done

Connection, information, recognition, experiences, freedom, growth. Each one is a destination that someone has been traveling toward for a long time, usually because of what they didn't have at the start.

We now hold all three layers. Next week we put them together into a single sentence.
