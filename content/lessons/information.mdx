Owen is buying a toaster.

There's a spreadsheet. It has eleven models on it, with columns for wattage, slot width, warranty length, and something called "browning consistency," taken from a forty-minute video review. He's read the manuals. He's read the one-star reviews, which he says are the only honest ones.

It's a toaster. It costs less than dinner. So what's actually going on?

## The end goal

Owen's value is **information**: mental preparedness, clarity, and control through understanding.

The equation running underneath is simple. **Knowledge equals safety.** If he understands a thing completely, it can't surprise him. And not being surprised matters to Owen a great deal.

## Where it often comes from

This value often grows out of **chaos or inconsistency** early in life, or from being told things that didn't match reality.

"Everything's fine," said in a house where things clearly weren't. Plans that changed without warning. Explanations that didn't add up. A child in that setting learns that what people say can't be relied on, and that the only real protection is to find out for themselves.

## How it shows up

- Many follow-up questions, then one more.
- "Just one more resource" before a decision.
- Research done before anything is bought, booked, or agreed.
- A visible reaction to three things: **vagueness**, **surprises**, and **withholding**.

That last one is worth underlining. For an information person, "don't worry about the details" is about the most worrying sentence there is.

## What to listen for

<Phrases>
- "I don't like walking in blind."
- "I just want to know what I'm getting into."
- "Ultimately, I just want to understand how it works."
</Phrases>

In the adjectives: they admire people who are *prepared*, *thorough*, *sharp*, *straight with you*.

## How to move them

We give them **mental security**.

That means walking them through exactly how the thing works. Step one, step two, what happens if step two goes wrong. We inform with precision.

And then we do something that feels backward: **we let them come to us**.

An information person can't be hurried into a decision. Pushing reads as hiding something. If we lay everything out clearly and step back, they'll finish their own checking and return when they're satisfied. The stepping back is part of the pitch.

## What to avoid

- "Trust me."
- "We'll figure out the details later."
- Rounding off. If the answer is 37, we don't say "about 40."
- Pretending to know. "I don't know, but I'll find out" earns more credit with Owen than a confident guess. A wrong answer he later catches will cost us everything.

## Information or intelligence?

These are easy to mix up, because both involve knowing things.

**Intelligence** is a need. It's about being *seen as* smart by other people. It needs an audience.

**Information** is a value. It's about *having* the understanding, whether or not anyone's watching. Owen will never show anyone that spreadsheet. He didn't make it to impress us. He made it so the toaster couldn't let him down.

A quick tell: the intelligence need corrects us. The information value asks us another question.

## Living alongside it

The eleven-toaster spreadsheet can be exhausting from the outside. It's worth remembering what it gives the rest of us.

The information person is the one who read the contract. Who knows where the exits are. Who noticed the clause everyone else skipped. When things go sideways, they've usually already thought about it.
