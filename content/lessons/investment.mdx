Seven seasons in, the show is terrible, and Grace knows it.

The good writers left after season four. Her favorite character was written out. She spends most episodes on her phone. And when her sister suggests they watch something else, Grace says, with real feeling, "I can't stop now. I've watched ninety hours of this."

Ninety hours she can't get back are, somehow, the reason to spend ten more.

## How investment decides

Grace uses the **investment** pillar. She makes choices based on what she's already put in, and what she expects to get back.

The currency can be almost anything: time, money, energy, emotion, loyalty. What matters is the ledger. Before deciding, she checks the balance.

This makes investment deciders prone to what economists call the sunk-cost fallacy: continuing with something because of what it's already cost. From the outside it can look stubborn. From the inside it feels like integrity. *I'm someone who finishes things.*

## What it sounds like

<Phrases>
- "It would be a waste to stop."
- "I can't walk away now."
- "I want to see it through."
- "I've already put so much into this."
</Phrases>

Listen for the tense. Investment speaks in the **past**: what's been done, given, sacrificed. The story of the effort comes before any talk about the future.

## What it can look like

We might notice tension when change comes up: a tightening jaw, arms crossing. Long-kept objects and memorabilia. A habit of stalling before agreeing to anything new.

These are weak, general hints. Plenty of people cross their arms because they're cold. The past-tense justification is the reliable signal.

## How to frame it

The worst thing we can do with an investment decider is wave away what they've built. "Forget all that, let's start fresh" tells them the ledger is being wiped. They'll defend it.

So we frame our idea as **honoring and paying off the investment**. They're someone who finishes, and this is how the effort finally pays.

- "You've already done the hard part. This gets you the return you deserve."
- "Everything you've built makes this possible."
- "This protects what you've put in."

**What to avoid:** minimizing past effort, and any framing that sounds like *starting over* or *walking away*.

## An example

Suppose a friend has spent six years on a business that isn't working, and we think she should change direction.

"Maybe it's time to cut your losses" is honest, and it will go nowhere. She'll hear six years being called a loss.

Try instead: "You've spent six years learning this market. Nobody starting today has that. What if those six years were the foundation for the next version?"

The change of direction is the same. But now the six years are an asset being put to use, and she can say yes without betraying them.

## Investment or conformity?

Both can say "we've always done it this way," so they sometimes get confused.

Ask what's being protected. **Conformity** protects the *rule*: it's the proper way. **Investment** protects the *effort*: think of everything we've put in.

If Grace's show had been cancelled after one episode, she wouldn't mind. Walter, from the conformity lesson, would keep watching a new show only if it's what people watch.

## The gift

It's worth saying that investment deciders are the people who are still there in year six. They keep promises. They finish. In a world of abandoned projects, that's rarer than it should be.

Our job is to make sure their loyalty gets pointed at something that can pay it back.
