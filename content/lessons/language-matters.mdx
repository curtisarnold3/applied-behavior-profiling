A manager gives two of her people the same compliment on the same afternoon: "You're so thoughtful. Everyone loves working with you."

The first one glows for the rest of the day. The second nods, says thanks, and goes back to his desk looking faintly insulted.

Same words. Same sincerity. So why did one land and the other bounce?

## What we're all chasing

Start with a little chemistry.

Human beings chase the brain's reward chemicals: oxytocin, dopamine, serotonin, endorphins. And one of the most reliable ways to get them is to have a social need met. Feeling included. Feeling respected. Feeling seen.

That means other people are, for each of us, a main source of feeling good.

And what carries that reward from one person to another? Mostly, words.

## Language is the delivery system

Think of language as a delivery truck. The reward is the package.

When our words match what a person is hungry for, the package arrives and the brain lights up. When they don't match, the truck pulls up to the wrong house. The package might be lovely. Nobody's home to sign for it.

Our words either unlock the reward or shut it off. There isn't much middle ground.

## Why most persuasion fails

Here's the claim at the heart of this lesson. Most persuasion doesn't fail because the message is bad. It fails because **the right message goes to the wrong kind of person.**

- Data, delivered to someone who needed empathy.
- Praise, delivered to someone who needed respect.
- Logic, delivered to someone who needed approval.

That's what happened with the manager. Her first employee wanted to be liked, and "everyone loves working with you" was exactly the package. Her second wanted to be respected for his competence. He heard "you're nice," and it felt like being patted on the head.

## The mistake we all make

Why do we keep sending the wrong package?

Because we tend to speak from *our own* need. Someone who wants to feel smart assumes everybody wants to feel smart, so they hand out compliments about cleverness. Someone who wants to belong assumes everyone does, so they tell the lone wolf how well he fits in.

We give what we'd like to get. It's generous, and it misses.

It's a bit like buying a gift. The lazy way is to buy what we'd want. The good way is to pay attention to what *they* keep looking at in the shop window.

## The formula

So here's the working formula for the rest of the course:

> Read the need. Match the language. Deliver the reward.

Three steps. The first is observation, the second is communication, and all of it leans on the Success Triangle.

There's a popular idea that influence is a numbers game: talk to enough people and some will say yes. This turns that around. It's not a numbers game. It's a brain game. One well-matched sentence does more than twenty well-meant ones.

## A quick test

Try this one. A pitch packed with statistics falls flat with a listener who mostly wants their hardship acknowledged. Why?

Because numbers aren't the reward that listener is after. They wanted to hear, "That sounds really hard, and I believe you." The statistics may all be true. They went to the wrong house.

<Callout label="Why this is the ethical version">
Matching language isn't a trick for getting our way. It's the difference between talking at someone and actually reaching them. We're giving people something real that they've been reaching for, and we only say what we mean.
</Callout>

## Where this takes us

We now have the whole foundation.

People see through a lens, and we can climb to a better one. Behavior comes from childhood strategies. Everyone is masked and hurting. And words are how we reach the person behind the mask.

What we don't have yet is the list. *Which* needs? What does each one sound like? What's the matching language?
