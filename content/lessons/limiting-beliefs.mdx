A young elephant is tied to a wooden stake with a light rope. It pulls and pulls, and the stake holds.

Years later the elephant weighs four tons. It could uproot the stake without noticing. The rope is the same light rope.

It doesn't pull. It learned, a long time ago, that pulling doesn't work, and it hasn't checked since.

Most of us have a stake or two like that. We call them limiting beliefs: conclusions we reached once, usually young, and never went back to test.

## Why this comes last

This week we've rated our authority and found the lowest trait. The natural next question is: *why is it low?*

Often the answer is a sentence. "I'm just not a confident person." "People like me don't get those jobs." "I've never been good with money."

A plan to raise a trait won't hold if there's a belief underneath saying it can't be raised. So before the plan, we look for the rope.

## Two ground rules

**No witch hunts.** We're not trying to interrogate every thought we've ever had. Beliefs surface on their own if we give them room. Hunting for them just makes us anxious.

**Have a strategy for testing them.** Noticing a belief isn't enough. We need a way to check whether it's true, and that's the four questions below.

## Where they hide: inside

Some limiting beliefs show up as a general state before they show up as a sentence. A few themes are worth checking against our own lives.

- **Going through the motions.** Days get done, but we're not really in them.
- **Having to win.** Being wrong feels unbearable, so every disagreement becomes a contest.
- **Old family business.** Something from home that still stings and never got settled.
- **Not knowing what we stand for,** or knowing, and living at odds with it.
- **Holding on.** A person, a role, or a story about ourselves we can't put down.
- **No picture of it going well.** We can imagine failing in detail and succeeding not at all.
- **Running on fumes.** Constant stress, tension around other people, health pushed to the bottom of the list.

If one of those gets a small flinch of recognition, there's probably a belief sitting under it.

## Where they hide: outside

Others reveal themselves in how we react to **other people**. Finishing these sentences quickly, without editing, is a good way to catch them.

- "When I watch someone ask for a raise without flinching, I assume they..."
- "When someone in the room outranks me, I..."

Whatever came out first is worth a look. If the answer was "...are greedy," what does that suggest we believe about asking for money ourselves?

It also helps to scan the five areas where these beliefs tend to live: **money, relationships, health, work, and self**.

## The four test questions

Once we've got a belief written down, we put it through four questions.

**1. Do I know an exception?** One real counter-example is enough to show a belief isn't a law. "People like me don't get those jobs." Do we know of even one who did?

**2. Is this belief helping or limiting my life?** Not "is it true?" but "what is it doing for me?" Some beliefs protect us. Many just keep us in the yard.

**3. Does it put me in charge?** A limiting belief usually hands control to something outside us: other people, luck, the past. A useful belief gives the wheel back.

**4. What would a good coach tell me?** Picture someone experienced, kind, and unimpressed by excuses. What would they say?

## Two exercises

**The mental exercise.** List the beliefs. For each, trace it to its source: when did we first decide this, and who was in the room? Then write its **opposite**. Finally, talk to our past self the way an objective friend would, someone who can see what the kid couldn't.

**The emotional exercise.** Go back to the event the belief came from, and compare how it felt *then* with how it feels *now*. At eight, being laughed at in class felt like the end of the world. At forty, we can see a room of nervous eight-year-olds. The event hasn't changed. Our size has.

That's the elephant noticing how big it's grown.

## Writing the opposite

The opposite belief isn't a cheerful slogan. "I'm amazing with money!" won't survive the afternoon.

A good opposite is something we can actually believe and act on. "I've never been good with money" becomes "I can learn to manage money, one habit at a time." It's modest. It's true. And it puts us in charge.
