New Haven, Connecticut, in the early 1960s. A man answers a newspaper ad for a study on memory at Yale and is shown into a laboratory.

A researcher in a gray lab coat explains the task. The volunteer will be the "teacher." In the next room is a "learner," strapped to a chair with an electrode on his wrist. Each time the learner gets a word pair wrong, the teacher is to press a switch and give him an electric shock, moving one switch higher each time.

The switches run in a long row, with labels that climb from mild to severe.

The volunteer begins. The learner makes mistakes. The switches go up.

## What happened

The study was run by the psychologist Stanley Milgram, and he published it in 1963.

The shocks weren't real, and the learner was part of the research team. The volunteer didn't know that. As far as he could tell, he was hurting a stranger.

When volunteers hesitated, the experimenter didn't shout or threaten. He calmly told them to continue.

And **a majority continued all the way to the highest level on the board.**

These weren't unusual people. They were ordinary men from the local area who'd answered an ad. Many were visibly distressed. They went on anyway.

## What it shows

The finding is simple and uncomfortable: ordinary people defer to perceived authority far more readily than we'd like to think.

It's tempting to read about it and feel sure we'd have been the one who refused. Most of the volunteers would have said the same thing beforehand.

## What made the authority believable

For our purposes, the useful question is *why* the experimenter was obeyed. Four things stand out.

**The setting.** A laboratory at a respected university. Before a word was spoken, the room said: serious people do serious work here.

**The role.** The lab coat, the clipboard, the title. The volunteer wasn't taking instructions from a man. He was taking them from a scientist.

**Calm insistence.** The experimenter never raised his voice. He was steady and certain. We met this in the self-assessment: ease reads as strength.

**Shifted responsibility.** The experimenter made it clear that the responsibility was his. Once the volunteer felt he was only carrying out instructions, something inside him let go.

Look at that list again with the Authority Triangle in mind. Setting, appearance, composure. These are the outward signs of authority, and they worked on nearly everyone, whether or not there was anything trustworthy behind them.

## Why this is in Week 8

We've spent this week learning how to build authority. Milgram is here to show what authority *does*.

It moves people. It moves them past their own judgment, and past their own discomfort. That's exactly why it's worth having, and exactly why it's dangerous.

If we do the work in this unit, people will start to listen to us more readily. Some of them will go along with us when they shouldn't. They'll assume we've thought it through. They'll set their own doubts aside because we seemed sure.

That isn't a reason to stay small. It's a reason to be careful.

## The responsibility

So here's what the experiment asks of anyone who's building authority.

**Make it safe to disagree.** The volunteers who stopped were often the ones who found a way to say no out loud. We can make that easier for the people around us: "Tell me if you think I'm wrong. I mean it."

**Don't hide behind the role.** "It's policy" and "I'm the expert" are the lab coat talking. If we can't explain why, we shouldn't be insisting.

**Keep the responsibility.** When people act on our word, what happens next is partly ours.

**Notice our own deference.** The experiment is also about us as followers. When a confident person in a well-appointed room tells us to continue, that's the moment to check with our own judgment.
