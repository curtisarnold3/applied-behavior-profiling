Hank's phone is eight years old.

The screen has a crack across one corner. The battery lasts until about two in the afternoon. His kids have offered, begged, and finally bought him a new one, which is still in its box on the hall table.

"This one works," Hank says. And he's right. It makes calls. When it stops making calls, he'll open the box. Not a day sooner.

## How necessity decides

Hank uses the **necessity** pillar. He makes choices by one test: *is this actually required?*

What moves him is urgency, immediacy, the thing that must be done. He responds to deadlines and consequences. He doesn't respond to novelty, and he doesn't respond to comfort. "It would be nicer" isn't a reason. "It's broken" is.

## What it sounds like

<Phrases>
- "We can't afford to wait."
- "Let's cut the fluff."
- "What's the bottom line?"
- "I don't have time to mess around."
- "Okay, but what are we doing?"
</Phrases>

The tone is sharp and results-driven. Small talk gets skipped. If we've been explaining for more than a minute, we'll start to feel Hank waiting for us to arrive somewhere.

## What it can look like

We might see purposeful movement and a no-nonsense posture. There's skepticism toward anything new, and restlessness when the conversation turns theoretical.

As always, treat these as general hints, weaker than speech. Some people simply walk fast. The justification, "do we actually need this?", is what we're listening for.

## How to frame it

We frame the idea in terms of **efficiency, immediacy, and consequence**. Action isn't optional, and our solution takes away delay, risk, or waste.

- "Doing nothing costs more than doing the right thing now."
- "This needs to happen before the end of the month, and here's why."
- "It saves you the two hours a week you're losing."

There's a subtlety, though. Necessity deciders like directness, and they dislike being told what to do. So we deliver the direct message through a soft frame: "You might want to consider..." followed by something plain and blunt.

## Three ways to lose them

**Over-explaining.** They wanted the bottom line three sentences ago.

**Rambling.** Background, context, and the story of how we got here all sound like fluff.

**Too many options.** This is the big one. Hand a necessity decider five choices and they **freeze**. None of the five has been shown to be *required*, so none gets picked. One clear recommendation, or two at most, works far better.

## An example

We'd like Hank to replace the office's aging server.

The novelty pitch, "the new ones are amazing," means nothing to him. The conformity pitch, "everyone's upgraded," means nearly as little.

The necessity pitch: "You might want to look at this. The server's out of security updates in March. If it fails after that, we're down for a week. One replacement fits; it takes a day to install."

A deadline, a consequence, one option. He'll have it ordered by lunch.

## Necessity or investment?

Both can look like refusing to change, so they're worth telling apart.

**Investment** keeps the old thing because of what's been put into it. Grace, from the last lesson, keeps watching because of ninety hours.

**Necessity** keeps the old thing because nothing has made a change required. Hank has no feelings about his phone at all. The day it dies, he'll replace it without a backward glance.

One is about the past. The other is about whether there's a reason *right now*.

## All six, once more

That's the last pillar. Deviance breaks the pattern. Novelty chases the new. Social protects the circle. Conformity follows the rules. Investment honors the ledger. Necessity does what's required.
