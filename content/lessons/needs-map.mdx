Three people get into an elevator on a Monday morning, and somebody asks the question we all ask: "Good weekend?"

The first says, "Closed on the lake house, actually. Youngest buyer the agent's ever had." The second says, "Oh, you know. Did the team hike. It was nice to be included." The third says, "Fine! Sorry, I'm probably holding everyone up, aren't I?"

Same elevator, same question, three completely different answers. And none of them were really about the weekend.

## What we're actually hearing

Each of those people told us what they're hoping to get from the rest of us. One wants to be seen as exceptional. One wants to belong. One wants to hear she's doing okay.

That's the idea this whole unit rests on. In nearly every conversation, a person is chasing something from the people around them. We'll call that a **need**: the emotional fuel they're after right now.

It's hiding in ordinary words. We've been hearing it our whole lives. We just haven't had a map.

## Three tools, one at a time

This course gives us three tools, and they stack.

- **Needs** are what someone is chasing from other people.
- **Decision pillars** are how they justify their choices. That's Unit 3.
- **Values** are the end goal underneath it all. That's Unit 4.

We start with needs because they're the loudest. Once our ears are tuned, they're hard to miss.

## The six needs

<NeedsMap />

There are six. Three are **primary**: significance, acceptance, and approval. Three are **secondary**: intelligence, pity, and strength.

| Need | What they want from us |
| --- | --- |
| Significance | To be seen as exceptional |
| Acceptance | To belong |
| Approval | To be judged good and kind |
| Intelligence | To be seen as smart |
| Pity | To have their hardship believed |
| Strength | Respect |

Each one gets its own short lesson. For now, we just need the shape of the map.

## This isn't a personality quiz

Here's where people go wrong first. They hear "six needs" and start sorting everyone they know into six boxes, permanently.

Don't. We're not labeling people, and we're not doing a psychic act. We're noticing what someone is reaching for in this conversation.

If we spot one need, great. If we spot two, we speak to both. People don't fit neatly in a spreadsheet, and they don't have to.

## The golden rule: read the moment

Think back to being nine years old. We were one kid in the classroom, another on the playground, and a third at home. Same child, three different strategies, because each place rewarded something different.

Nobody grows out of that. The classroom becomes work. The playground becomes our social life. Home stays home.

So the same woman can chase significance in a meeting and acceptance at her book club, and both are real. Our job isn't to work out which one is the "true" her. Our job is to read what's showing here, now, with us.

<Callout label="Field rule">
Read the moment, not the life story. Profile what a person is showing in this context.
</Callout>

## Words beat wardrobe

We'll mention things we can see in these lessons: clothes, posture, what someone carries. They're worth a glance.

But they're weak signals. A logo or a slouch is a general hint at best, and plenty of people dress the way they do for reasons that have nothing to do with us. What a person says is much stronger evidence than what they wear. When the two disagree, we trust the words.

There's one question that makes any display more useful, whether it's a watch, a shirt, or a bumper sticker:

> What does this person want me to think about them because of this?

We don't answer it out loud. We hold it, and then we listen.

## It's a skill, like driving

Remember the first week behind the wheel? Mirrors, pedals, signals, the terrifying merge. Every part needed conscious effort, and it was exhausting.

Then one day we drove home and realized we hadn't thought about any of it.

Reading needs works the same way. It'll feel clunky for the first couple of weeks. We'll catch a need ten minutes after the conversation ended and kick ourselves. That's the skill arriving, just a little late.

Keep logging what we hear. The delay shrinks.
