Sam has had four hobbies this year.

In January it was sourdough. By March the starter was dead and there was a bouldering membership. June brought a film camera. It's now autumn, the camera's in a drawer, and Sam is telling us, eyes bright, about a beekeeping course.

It would be easy to call this flaky. But watch what happens when Sam talks about the bees. That energy is real. It just has a short half-life.

## How novelty decides

Sam uses the **novelty** pillar: choices get made according to what's new, changing, and exciting. The best option is the *next* one.

It isn't indecision. It's a clear and consistent rule. Given two choices, the one Sam hasn't tried wins.

## What it sounds like

<Phrases>
- "What else is out there?"
- "I get bored so easily."
- "Let's try something different."
- "I hate doing the same thing twice."
- "I need something fresh."
- "I've never tried that."
</Phrases>

Listen to the delivery as well as the words. Novelty tends to talk fast, with rising intonation, and it hops between topics. A conversation with Sam covers a lot of ground.

## What it can look like

We might see eyes that scan the room, fidgeting when things get dull, and the newest gear or the latest trend.

As ever, these are soft signals. Some people fidget because the chair is uncomfortable. What people say about why they chose something tells us much more.

## How to frame it

For a novelty decider, the words "tried and true" land like a door closing.

So we frame the idea as **an adventure, an experiment, a new experience**. We lead with what's different and brand new.

- "Nobody's really done it this way yet."
- "Here's the twist most people don't know about."
- "Want to be the first to try it?"

And we **match their energy**. A fresh idea delivered in a flat voice doesn't sound fresh. We don't need to bounce off the walls, but we do need to sound like we find it interesting too.

**What to avoid:** routine, heavy structure, long lists of steps, and any praise for how long something has been around.

## An example

Say we want Sam to commit to a twelve-week training program.

Twelve weeks of the same thing is, for Sam, a prison sentence. If we describe it as "a proven, structured routine," we've lost before we've finished the sentence.

So we find what's true and new about it: "Every two weeks it switches. Different focus, different format. Week five is something I've never seen in a program before."

We haven't lied about the twelve weeks. We've shown Sam the part of it that speaks to how Sam decides.

## Novelty or deviance?

Here's the pair again, from the other side.

Both will reject the ordinary choice, so they look alike from a distance. The question that separates them is: *ordinary compared to what?*

**Novelty** compares to the past. Is this new *to me*? Sam doesn't care if a million other people keep bees. Sam hasn't.

**Deviance** compares to the crowd. Is this what *they* do? A deviance decider would lose interest in beekeeping the moment it got popular.

A quick test we can run in conversation: mention something brand new that everyone has started talking about. Novelty leans in. Deviance leans out.

## Living with it

One last thought, since many of us work or live with a novelty decider.

The dead sourdough starter isn't a character flaw. It's the cost of a mind that's built to find the next thing. That same wiring is why Sam is often the first to spot an opportunity everyone else walked past.
