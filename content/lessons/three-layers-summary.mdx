Ask three people why their friend Beth just bought a camper van, and we'll get three answers.

"She wants everyone to see she's not like the rest of us." "She saw one on a trip and had to try it." "She's wanted to get out from under her schedule for years."

Who's right? Possibly all three. They're each describing a different layer of the same decision.

## Three layers

Here's the whole model in one place.

**Needs** are the emotional fuel a person is chasing from other people. Significance, acceptance, approval, intelligence, pity, strength.

**Decision pillars** are the reasoning filter: how a person chooses and how they justify the choice. Deviance, novelty, social, conformity, investment, necessity.

**Values** are the deep goal, often one that went unmet. Connection, information, recognition, experiences, freedom, growth.

Back to Beth. *Wanting to be seen as unlike the rest* is a need: significance. *Had to try it* is a pillar: novelty. *Out from under her schedule* is a value: freedom.

One van. Three layers.

## How the layers relate

It helps to picture a journey.

The **value** is the destination, typed into the map years ago. The **pillar** is the GPS, choosing each turn. The **need** is the fuel that keeps the engine running along the way.

They don't predict each other. Knowing someone's need doesn't tell us their pillar, and knowing the pillar doesn't tell us the value. Each has to be heard separately. That's why we learned to listen in three different places.

| Layer | What it is | Where we hear it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Need | What they want from people | Repetitions: what they keep reaching for |
| Pillar | How they decide | Justifications: what they explain or defend |
| Value | Where they're headed | Adjectives: what they admire in others |

## Two is fine, at every layer

This rule has followed us since week three, and it applies to all three layers.

If we hear two needs, we speak to both. Two pillars, we frame for both. Two values, we connect to both.

People aren't spreadsheets with one cell per column. Beth may decide by novelty and a bit of deviance. That's not a failed read. That's a more accurate one.

## Pattern recognition, not perfection

Here's a worry worth putting down right now: *what if I get it wrong?*

We will. Regularly. So does everyone who does this well.

The model isn't a test with an answer key. It's pattern recognition, and patterns firm up over many observations. A first read is a hypothesis. We offer a little matching language, we watch what happens, and we adjust.

A wrong guess, delivered kindly, costs almost nothing. If we speak to significance and it turns out to be approval, the person simply heard something generous that didn't quite land. We try again.

## Where to practice

Anywhere there are words.

- **Texts.** Look at the last message from a friend. What's the justification in it?
- **Calls.** What does this person keep coming back to?
- **Emails.** Which adjectives did they pick?
- **Meetings.** Who's defending what, and how?
- **Relationships.** What has our partner been telling us, in different words, for years?

The last one tends to be the most humbling, and the most worthwhile.

## What's next

We have the three layers as three separate reads. The next lesson joins them.

There's a single sentence that holds a person's need, pillar, and value together and shows how each one feeds the next. Once we can build it, we've got a working profile in a dozen words.
