# UNIT 1 — FOUNDATIONS (Weeks 1–2)

## 1.1 The Five Lenses → Five Stages of Perception

**Core idea:** How you see other people determines what you can read in them, and what results you get in life. The original "lenses" are really stages of development.

**The five stages**

| Stage | You see others as... | Signature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Separation and reactivity | Broken | Ego runs the show; constant judgment and comparison; defend or attack; world feels hostile; little self-awareness |
| 2. Comparison and judgment | Different | Notices nuance, but differences still irritate; curiosity starting, ego still filtering it |
| 3. Observation and detachment | Facts | Steps back, less reactive; people's situations are patterns and history, not personal; comparison becomes intellectual |
| 4. Empathy and understanding | Reasons | Every behavior, even harmful, has a reason (pain, conditioning, history); compassion becomes a byproduct rather than a technique |
| 5. Unity and reflection | Reflections | Others mirror your own inner world; judgment largely gone; every interaction is self-awareness |

**Key concepts**
- Stage 4 is about *them*; stage 5 is about *you*. At stage 5, what you notice in others says as much about you as about them.
- **Life as an inkblot test:** perception is a prediction your brain builds from your past (neuroscientist Anil Seth calls it a "controlled hallucination"). Two people see the same event differently because of what they bring to it. Media diet shapes this too.
- Profiling use: when someone treats a small inconvenience as a crisis, their reaction tells you more about their inner state (often control, anxiety, trauma, or insecurity) than about the event.
- Perspective is trainable: under stress, "zoom out." The goal is to make the higher stage who you are, not a technique you remember to use.

**The Six Wound Types** (taught in the extended lecture)

| Wound | Loss behind it | How it forms | Adult signs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Social | Loss of acceptance | Excluded, rejected, bullied, always the new kid | Echoes others' opinions, agrees before you finish, avoids conflict, over-apologizes, fishes for reassurance, anxious about being left out |
| Authority | Loss of control | Either micromanaged or left with no structure | Strong reactions to criticism from authority, challenges rules, needs control, stresses independence, drifts into leadership or control roles |
| Emotional | Abandonment or betrayal | Let down repeatedly, a parent who left, friends who turned away | Guarded, dodges personal questions, keeps relationships shallow or on their terms, avoids commitment, jokes when things get deep. Theme: "don't hurt me" |
| Security | Loss of safety and stability | Unstable home, money trouble, volatile caregivers, frequent moves or deployments | Worst-case thinking, avoids risk and novelty, obsesses over financial security, hyper-aware of small threats, stressed by disrupted routine. Helps to let them predict the future |
| Validation | Loss of recognition | Praised only for grades or performance | Brings up achievements unprompted, seeks praise from authority, crushed by small criticism, "nobody recognizes what I do," uneasy out of the spotlight |
| Trust | Fear of vulnerability | Caregivers who lied or broke promises | Rarely shares personal matters, keeps people at arm's length, "they have to earn my trust," tests loyalty, repeats rehearsed "vulnerable-sounding" lines. Theme: "don't lie to me." Hardest to unwind |

- People carry more than one wound. Triage like an ER: start with the one driving the behavior you most want to change.
- Healing goal: turn a wound into a scar. A wound needs attention; a scar doesn't. Track it regularly, without self-judgment.

**Practice:** journal three moments of irritation this week; label the stage you were in and restate the other person as a reason.

**Quiz seeds:** match stage to "sees others as"; match an adult behavior description to a wound; "What's the difference between the emotional and trust wound?" (don't hurt me vs. don't lie to me).

## 1.2 The Success Triangle

**Core idea:** Any success or failure — a sale, a relationship, a leadership moment — comes down to three trainable skills. If results are off, one side is leaking.

| Side | What it covers | What failure looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Self-mastery | Confidence, discipline, focus, emotional control, posture, tone, timing, presence | Overreacting, fear-based responses, weak boundaries, insecurity leaking, right words delivered the wrong way |
| Communication | What you say, how, and how the other person feels and decides in response | Good intentions landing as pressure or confusion; data to someone who needs feeling; right thing, wrong person, wrong time |
| Observation | Decoding what's actually happening: cues, tension, unconscious patterns | Missing discomfort or disengagement, missing readiness to decide, misreading needs or values, trusting the wrong person |

- Every later skill in the course strengthens one of the three sides.
- Diagnostic habit: when something goes wrong, check the three sides first.

**Practice:** take one interaction that went badly; name the leaking side and what you'd change.

**Quiz seeds:** classify five failure scenarios by side.

## 1.3 The Childhood Development Triangle

**Core idea:** Children build their behavior around three needs — **friends**, **safety**, and **rewards**. Where one was missing, the child adapts, and the adaptation becomes adult behavior.

- **Friends:** what did I have to do to earn and keep friends, belong, feel loved?
- **Safety:** what did I have to do to feel emotionally and physically safe?
- **Rewards:** what did I have to do to get praise, attention, validation from authority and peers?

| Shortfall | Adult strategies |
| --- | --- |
| Friends | People pleasers, chameleons, overachievers |
| Safety | Avoiders, hyper-controllers, chronic apologizers |
| Rewards | Approval seekers, performers, status chasers |

**Everyday examples:** can't take a compliment (praise was rare or followed by criticism); avoids all conflict (disagreement meant screaming or withdrawal); constant name-dropping (felt invisible); panics when plans change (unpredictable home, so control equals safety).

**Key line:** behavior is patterned, not personal. You're not seeing a difficult adult; you're seeing a child's survival strategy wearing a grown-up face. Self-understanding is the gateway to self-mastery.

**Practice:** match three people you know to the triangle side driving them; give one piece of evidence each.

**Quiz seeds:** "Someone over-explains everything to clients — which side?" (safety); "jokes during serious moments?" (friends).

## 1.4 The Four Laws of Human Behavior

1. **Everyone is suffering and insecure.** Nobody is "fine." The more put-together someone looks, the more armor they may have built. Seeing suffering as the default switches on empathy and stops you taking things personally.
2. **Everyone wears a mask.** The curated self we show to be acceptable and safe from judgment. It isn't fake — it's survival. Ancestrally, rejection from the tribe meant death. (Fear of public speaking is really fear of mass judgment.)
3. **Everyone pretends not to wear a mask.** We cling to the mask because removing it exposes shame, the most avoided human emotion.
4. **Everyone is a product of childhood suffering and reward.** Roughly by 12, most interpersonal behavior is set; by 18, it's locked in. Adults replay scripts written by their younger selves.

**Key concepts**
- Most of profiling (the course says about 80%) is discovering how a person hides shame and avoids judgment.
- Every mask is custom-built to shield the person from what they fear most: being not enough, too much, unworthy, or unlovable.
- Judgment equals failure: the moment you judge someone, you lose the ability to influence the situation. People don't want to be fixed; they want to be seen without judgment.

**Practice:** write down one judgment you made this week and rewrite it as a reason.

**Quiz seeds:** order the four laws; "Why does judgment cost you influence?"

## 1.5 How Language Matters More Than Ever

**Core idea:** Humans chase the brain's reward chemicals (oxytocin, dopamine, serotonin, endorphins), released when social needs are met. Language is the delivery system: your words either unlock that reward or shut it off.

- Most persuasion fails because the right message goes to the wrong kind of person: data to someone who needs empathy, praise to someone who needs respect, logic to someone who needs approval.
- People tend to speak from *their own* need (an intelligence-need person assumes everyone wants to feel smart). That's the common mistake.
- Formula: **read the need → match the language → deliver the reward.** It's not a numbers game; it's a brain game.
- Bridges directly into Unit 2 (the language shift for each need is in the Unit 2 table).

**Quiz seeds:** "A pitch full of statistics fails with a pity-need listener. Why?"

# UNIT 2 — HUMAN NEEDS (Weeks 3–4)

## 2.0 Introduction and the Human Needs Map (Core 01–02)

- Three tools in this course: **needs** (what someone is chasing from others), **decision pillars** (how they justify choices), **values** (their end goal). All three are hiding in ordinary words, pauses, and presentation.
- Six needs: **Significance, Acceptance, Approval** (primary) and **Intelligence, Pity, Strength** (secondary).
- Not a quiz, not psychic labeling. Spot one, great; spot two, address both.
- **Golden rule: read the moment.** People behaved differently in the classroom, playground, and home as kids; as adults those become work, social life, and home. Profile what's showing *in this context*, not a permanent type.
- It's a skill like driving: clunky first, automatic later.
- **Prioritize verbal indicators over visual ones.** Clothing and posture are weak, generalized signals; words are stronger.
- A useful question for any display (a shirt, a sticker, a watch): *What does this person want me to think about them because of this?*

## 2.1 Significance (Core 03)

- **Core desire:** to be seen as important, unique, impactful, or better than others. **Hidden fear:** being invisible or average.
- **Look for:** visible logos and luxury items, statement pieces, curated attention-getting outfits, immaculate grooming designed to be noticed; chin up, firm or exaggerated posture, louder than the group, name-dropping, humble brags, taking the "power seat."
- **Listen for:** "I was the first to...," "People always come to me when...," "I can't stand mediocrity," "They didn't even recognize what I did," "I have really high standards," "I'm kind of a perfectionist," "I don't want to feel average." Tone: competitive, prideful, slightly dismissive of others.
- **Language that lands:** impact, achievement, exceptionality — "At your level...," "Most people wouldn't even get what you're doing."
- **Superpower:** can tune out others' judgment and stay on mission.

## 2.2 Acceptance (Core 04)

- **Core desire:** to belong, fit in, be part of the group. Think *safety through connection*. **Hidden fear:** exclusion, rejection.
- **Look for:** trend-driven or tribe-matching clothing (company hoodies, team colors, group gear), avoids extremes; mirrors body language, laughs with the group, glances for others' reactions, stays physically close (proximity equals acceptance), self-edits in real time.
- **Listen for:** "I just didn't feel part of it," "They left me out," "I love being part of something bigger," "I want to find my people," "I hate being the odd one out," lots of *we / they / everyone*.
- **Language that lands:** tribal, inclusive, "we" language — "You're exactly the kind of person people want around."
- **Superpower:** chameleon-like fit, and fierce loyalty to their tribe.

## 2.3 Approval (Core 05)

- **Core desire:** to be praised, validated, liked, seen as *good*. A more compliment-hungry cousin of acceptance. Shorthand: *acceptance = I want to belong; approval = I need permission.*
- **Hidden fear:** disapproval, being seen as bad, letting someone down. Usually conflict-avoidant; overextends to keep others' good opinion.
- **Look for:** slightly overdressed, crisp and safe with a small "please like me" touch; over-nodding, over-complimenting, over-apologizing, laughing at weak jokes, self-deprecating humor to fish for reassurance, deflecting compliments while seeking them.
- **Listen for:** "I hope that makes sense," "Does that sound okay?," "I'm probably rambling," "I probably shouldn't say this, but...," "I'm just trying to help," "People say I'm easy to work with." Tone: softening, hedging.
- **Language that lands:** specific praise for specific behaviors — "You handled that beautifully," "I trust your judgment completely."
- If you can't tell acceptance from approval, treat it as both.
- **Superpower:** deep empathy.

## 2.4 Intelligence (Core 06)

- **Core desire:** to be *seen as* smart, competent, insightful (a need is what we want from others, not what we are). **Hidden fear:** being seen as stupid or uninformed.
- **Look for:** minimalist, neutral, carefully chosen clothing; intellectual signaling (clever tees, books on hand, gear); thinking poses, precise language, self-correction mid-sentence, starting sentences with "actually," citing studies and podcasts, detailed questions, slight emotional distance, may dismiss others when intellectually threatened.
- **Listen for:** "Well, technically...," "I read a study...," "Logically...," "I don't want to sound arrogant, but I've done a lot of research," "Most people don't understand this." Tone: precise, corrective, data-anchored.
- **Language that lands:** anchor in logic, strategy, insight — *theirs*. "You're the first one I thought of because this is complex." Praise their insight and watch them relax.
- **Superpower:** outstanding teachers.

## 2.5 Pity (Core 07)

- **Core desire:** to be cared for, nurtured, and have what they've been through recognized. A bid for control through helplessness. **Hidden fear:** being alone, unloved, or not believed.
- **Look for:** loose, layered, slightly disheveled or "cozy-sad" clothing; slouched, head forward of the heart; fidgeting, clinging to a mug or strap, sighing, soft passive language; stories where they're the one hurt or abandoned; appears drained.
- **Listen for:** "It's just been really hard lately," "People don't realize how much I'm juggling," "Nobody checks on me," "I don't want to be a burden, but...," "It's fine, I'm used to doing it alone."
- **Key insight:** they often resist solutions because the problem is the strategy. Engage with empathy, but don't get pulled into the black hole.
- **Language that lands:** empathy, safety, softness — "I don't know how you do it. You carry more than anyone realizes."
- **Superpower:** the course notes none clearly; possibly empathy.

## 2.6 Strength (Core 08)

- **Core desire:** to be seen as independent, tough, unshakable, in control — "untouchable." **Hidden fear:** being seen as weak or dependent.
- **Look for:** tough materials, tactical or utilitarian gear, dark colors, clothes that show muscle, power symbols; chest up, chin out, wide stance, takes up space, minimal expression, rare controlled laughter, shuts down emotional topics, "so what's the plan?", strong declaratives, interrupts; clean, short, controlled grooming.
- **Listen for:** "I handle my own business," "I don't need anybody's help," "I've been through worse," "People need to toughen up," "I don't show weakness," "I take care of mine." Tone: firm, restrained, stoic; aggressive if challenged.
- **Key insight:** control is their armor. They want respect over love, action over talk. Never approach soft or uncertain; they respect clarity, competence, boldness.
- **Language that lands:** respect, resilience, independence — "I trust your judgment," "You're one of the most solid people I know." **Ask permission before complimenting**; asking their advice is powerful.
- **Superpower:** fearlessness.

## 2.7 Needs Recap (Core 09)

- **Why these six:** each maps to something that, in an ancestral tribe, determined survival or reproduction (e.g., a sick person nobody cared for would die — pity).
- The six were chosen because they can be *profiled* and *used*, not because they're a complete list of human needs.
- **Superpowers:** approval → empathy; acceptance → chameleon fit and tribal loyalty; intelligence → teaching; strength → fearlessness; significance → immunity to others' judgment; pity → none clear.

## 2.8 Four Needs Circles (Core 10)

| Circle | Who | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Inner | Partners, mentors, very close family | Raw needs live here; often matches the need shown to parents |
| 2. Legacy | Long-time friends, your tribe | Why someone acts differently out with friends than at home |
| 3. Proximity | Coworkers, acquaintances | Good relationship, not close |
| 4. Outer | Strangers | How they want to be seen by people they never speak to |

- The same person may show a different need in each circle, or the same one across all.
- Field rule: you don't need to know the circle. **Identify the need, test it, use it, move on.**

**Unit 2 practice:** log 10 need identifications a week with the exact tipping phrase; try one matching compliment live and note the reaction.

**Unit 2 quiz seeds:** classify 12 phrases by need; acceptance vs. approval discrimination items; "What's the strength person's hidden fear?"; "Why ask permission before complimenting a strength person?"

# UNIT 3 — DECISION PILLARS (Week 5)

## 3.0 Bridge and introduction (Core 11, 12, 14)

- **Needs are the fuel; decision pillars are the GPS.** A need is what someone emotionally chases; a pillar is how they choose the next move.
- Two people with the same need can decide in completely different ways. Validate the need perfectly but frame the offer through the wrong pillar, and you get resistance, confusion, or rejection.
- Reading pillars lets you tailor framing, predict objections, and guide decisions calmly.
- Intro device: picture six friends — the rule-breaker, the always-trying-something-new one, the group's heart, the rule-follower, the loyalist who won't quit a bad show, and the "okay, but what are we doing?" fixer.

## 3.1–3.6 The six pillars (Core 13, 15–20)

### Deviance
- **Driver:** breaking patterns and norms, standing apart, being bold or disruptive; transformation through contrast. Takes *pride* in it.
- **Look for:** unconventional style, tattoos, bold choices; off-menu orders, parking wherever; relaxed, leaning-back posture.
- **Listen for:** "I've never liked doing things the traditional way," "I don't care what everyone else is doing," "That's just the system talking," "They said I couldn't, so I did." Blunt, sarcastic, contrastive; plays devil's advocate.
- **Frame it as:** breaking the pattern, challenging the status quo. "Most people don't have the courage to try this — I figured you're not most people." "This isn't the usual route, but it works." **Avoid:** groupthink, tradition, "everyone's doing it."

### Novelty
- **Driver:** newness, change, excitement; the next thing. Differs from deviance: deviance wants *different from the crowd*, novelty wants *new*.
- **Look for:** scanning eyes, fidgeting when bored, newest gear and trends, topic-hopping.
- **Listen for:** "I need something fresh," "I hate doing the same thing twice," "Let's try something different," "I get bored so easily," "What else is out there?" Fast, rising intonation.
- **Frame it as:** an adventure, experiment, new experience; what's different and brand new. "Here's the twist nobody knows about yet." Match their energy. **Avoid:** "tried and true," routine, heavy structure.

### Social
- **Driver:** how the decision affects the people close to them (friends, family, team); harmony and relationships.
- **Look for:** warm eye contact, mirroring, soft open gestures, nodding, leaning in, watching others before responding.
- **Listen for:** "I want everyone comfortable with this," "I don't want to hurt anyone's feelings," "How will they feel about this?" Gentle; *we / us / our team*; softens bold opinions.
- **Frame it as:** connection, harmony, positive impact on others. "This will make your team feel more supported." Calm, collaborative tone. **Avoid:** cold data dumps, every-man-for-himself framing, pushing or over-excitement.

### Conformity
- **Driver:** rules, structure, the proper way, what others (the larger culture) do; predictability and doing what's right. The inverse of deviance. Social = small circle; conformity = larger culture.
- **Look for:** upright symmetrical posture, hands close, dresses to group norms, modest and pressed, follows posted rules (proud of parking inside the lines).
- **Listen for:** "That's just how it's done," "We've always done it this way," "What's the proper way?," "I just want to do the right thing," "Those are really popular right now." Structured, polite; cites authority, policy, tradition.
- **Frame it as:** standard, proven, best practice, respectable. "This is the gold standard in your field." **Avoid:** disruptive or anti-establishment language, change without structure.
- **Red line to deviance:** very high conformity often comes with proportionally strong private deviance (pressure → bigger release). Know it; never raise it.

### Investment
- **Driver:** ROI — what they've already put in (time, money, energy, emotion, loyalty) and what they'll get back. Prone to the sunk-cost fallacy.
- **Look for:** tension when change comes up (tight jaw, crossed arms), long-kept items and memorabilia, stalls before agreeing to anything new.
- **Listen for:** "I've already put so much into this," "I want to see it through," "I can't walk away now," "It would be a waste to stop." Past-tense, effort-and-sacrifice language.
- **Frame it as:** honoring and paying off their investment; they're someone who finishes. "You've already done the hard part — this gets you the return you deserve." **Avoid:** minimizing past effort; "starting over" or "walking away" framing.

### Necessity
- **Driver:** urgency, immediacy, what must be done; "is this actually required?" Responds to deadlines and consequences, not novelty or comfort. (Picture the person with an eight-year-old phone who won't upgrade until it dies.)
- **Look for:** purposeful movement, no-nonsense posture, skepticism toward new things, restless when talk turns theoretical.
- **Listen for:** "I don't have time to mess around," "What's the bottom line?," "Let's cut the fluff," "We can't afford to wait." Sharp, results-driven, skips small talk.
- **Frame it as:** efficiency, immediacy, consequence — action isn't optional, and your solution removes delay, risk, or waste. "Doing nothing costs more than doing the right thing now." Deliver directness through soft framing ("you might want to consider..."). **Avoid:** over-explaining, rambling, too many options (they freeze).

## 3.7 Decisions wrap-up (Core 21)

- Listen for pillars everywhere — even on a customer-service call. With practice the formula disappears and you adjust language automatically.
- Values are the next and final layer: the end goal a person will give things up for.

**Unit 3 practice:** write one real request three ways for three pillars; use the one that fits.

**Unit 3 quiz seeds:** classify justification statements; deviance vs. novelty and social vs. conformity discrimination items; "Which pillar freezes when given too many options?"; rewrite a pitch for Investment.

# UNIT 4 — VALUES AND INTEGRATION (Weeks 6–7)

## 4.0 Values introduction and overview (Core 22–23)

- **Values are the end game.** Needs drive behavior, pillars guide it, values are what people are unconsciously trying to earn, gain, or restore.
- Values are usually built from **deficit**, not abundance: what felt missing in formative years. A kid who never felt important becomes an adult chasing recognition; one who never got straight answers lives for information; one who felt trapped fights for freedom.
- Speaking to someone's value means saying something they've been trying to fix for years.
- Some values resemble needs (connection vs. acceptance) but they're different drivers: a need is a means; a value is the end goal.
- Intro device: six friends — the heart of the circle, the researcher, the one who notices whether you noticed, the adventurer, the non-committal one, the self-improver.

## 4.1–4.6 The six values (Core 24–29)

| Value | End goal | Where it often comes from | Shows up as | Listen for ("ultimately, I just want...") | How to move them |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Connection | Emotional closeness, belonging, being truly seen | Emotional distance growing up | Checks in often, long caring messages, wants everyone to belong; not about the spotlight | "How are you *really* doing?"; wanting people to feel cared for | Close the emotional distance; real presence, not pressure or surface talk |
| Information | Mental preparedness, clarity, control through understanding; knowledge equals safety | Chaos, inconsistency, or being told things that didn't match reality | Many follow-up questions, "one more resource," triggered by vagueness, surprises, withholding | "I just want to know what I'm getting into," "I don't like walking in blind" | Give mental security: walk them through exactly how it works; inform with precision and let them come to you |
| Recognition | To be seen, valued, and remembered; visibility and worth | Effort and presence that went unnoticed | Notices when effort isn't acknowledged; mentions how hard they've worked | "I just want someone to see the effort," "I don't need praise, I just want to know it mattered" | Speak to their **impact**, not their intention: "People will remember what you did here" |
| Experiences | A life that's felt: richness, story, intensity | Dull, rigid environments where spontaneity was shut down | Chases variety, travel, changes jobs, hobbies, style; biggest fear is feeling numb | "I'm not trying to have a perfect life, I'm trying to have a full one" | Offer feelings and memories, not facts; help them imagine the moment, not the outcome |
| Freedom | Autonomy, self-direction, unrestricted choice | Heavy control and restriction; being told who to be | Resists firm plans and commitments, feels trapped by groupthink; says yes more easily when no is clearly allowed | "I just want to live on my own terms," "I'm not trying to be difficult, I just don't want to feel boxed in" | Space, ownership, flexibility; never corner, over-schedule, or present something as a must |
| Growth | To evolve and become more | Stagnant environments where staying small was rewarded | Constant learning, goals, optimizing; leaves jobs or relationships once growth stops | "I just want to become the best version of myself," "I don't need it easy, I need to know I'm not stuck" | Speak to their **trajectory**, not their status; show progress and the next self |

- Recognition vs. significance: significance is a *need* (wanting to be seen as exceptional by others now); recognition is a *value* (the end goal of being seen and having it matter). Growth vs. recognition: speak to trajectory, not status.

## 4.7 Summary (Core 30)

- The three layers: **needs** (emotional fuel from others), **decision pillars** (the reasoning filter), **values** (the deep, often unmet goal).
- If you see two of anything, speak to both.
- It's pattern recognition, not perfection. Practice in texts, calls, emails, meetings, relationships.

## 4.8 Error correction: the profile sentence (Foundations 07, extended lecture + PDF)

- **First rule:** don't listen to *what* people say; listen for *why they felt the need to say it.*
- **The profile sentence (behavioral DNA):** *If I [decision], then people will [need], so I can finally [value].*
- **Three things to listen for** (each gives a "fuzzy" first read you then map to the model):
  1. **Adjectives → values.** What someone admires or romanticizes in others is what they were (or are) deprived of. "She's so grounded" → stability; "he's so real" → authenticity; "they're so free" → freedom.
  2. **Justifications → decision pillars.** When someone explains or defends a recurring behavior, you're hearing survival logic and what used to be dangerous. "I like to keep to myself" → silence equals safety; "I learned not to count on people" → hyper-independence, likely neglect.
  3. **Repetitions → needs.** What does this person keep trying to get from the world, even when it never works? Patterns repeat because the nervous system is loyal to survival, not success.
- **Worked examples (paraphrased):**
  - "I'm always the one people lean on... sometimes I wonder if anyone would check on me." → pillar: necessity (if I don't, it won't get done); need: significance (useful = lovable); value: connection. Sentence: *If I stay strong for everyone, they'll keep me close, so I can finally feel connected.*
  - "I don't like big groups... people expect you to be someone you're not." → pillar: deviance (anti-conformity as defense); need: acceptance (hidden fear of rejection); value: freedom.
  - "I'm always chasing the next big thing; if I stop, I feel like I'm falling behind." → pillar: novelty (outrunning stagnation); need: approval (movement earns praise); value: recognition.
  - "I can't relax unless I'm in control; let your guard down and people take advantage." → pillar: control/safety; value: information (to manage risk). Sentence: *If I stay in control, I won't get hurt, so I can finally feel prepared.*
- **Responding:** each need has a fear and a matching "nutrient" message (significance: you matter for who you are, not what you do; acceptance: you belong here as you are; approval: nothing about you needs fixing; intelligence: your mind sees what others miss; pity: I believe you, your story deserves honoring; strength: ask permission first, then acknowledge the power you see). Delivery should be felt, not analyzed.
- **Positive association (3 steps):** (1) a trait they admire, (2) the behavior you want, (3) link the two. Example: for a self-reliant strength profile, link openness to the elite leaders they admire, so openness becomes a mark of strength.
- **Negative dissociation:** link the unwanted behavior to a type of person they look down on (e.g., "idea people" who never execute), then set them apart from it.
- **The reference PDF** lists commonly confused statements for every need, pillar, and value (e.g., what looks like strength but is approval). Use it to build the "commonly confused" quiz bank.
- **Ethics:** power with empathy makes you a good person; power without it is the alternative. Use these tools to help.

## 4.9 Common mistakes (Foundations 08)

- The original lesson is a short introduction to the lecture above plus the reference PDF. Treat it as: misreading acceptance as approval, reading permanent types instead of the moment, speaking from your own need, and confusing similar categories. Content comes from the error-correction PDF.

## 4.10 Wrap-up (Core 32)

- Close the course on the ethic: these tools are for building people up, not manipulating them. (The original lesson is mostly a sales pitch for further courses; don't port that part.)

**Unit 4 practice:** five profile sentences from real conversations, each with one alternative read ruled out.

**Unit 4 quiz seeds:** classify "ultimately, I just want..." statements by value; recognition vs. significance and growth vs. recognition items; build a profile sentence from a quoted statement; "Adjectives reveal ___; justifications reveal ___; repetitions reveal ___."

# UNIT 5 — AUTHORITY AND SELF-DEVELOPMENT (Week 8)

## 5.1 Authority self-assessment (based on the Hughes Authority Behavior Inventory)

- **Five traits**, each on five levels from struggling (1) to contagious (5): **Confidence, Discipline, Leadership, Gratitude, Enjoyment.**
  - Confidence: level 1 can't start conversations or take compliments; level 5 converses with anyone, takes criticism well, needs no reassurance.
  - Discipline: level 1 neglects tasks and routines; level 5 forms habits easily, plans and executes, prioritizes goals over enjoyment.
  - Leadership: level 1 blames others, takes credit, puts people down; level 5 never takes credit for the team's wins, lifts others, honest and tactful.
  - Gratitude: level 1 sees the negative in everything; level 5 expresses appreciation constantly and creates grateful environments.
  - Enjoyment: level 1 thinks people are out to get them; level 5 is the calm "rock" with a magnetic sense of well-being.
- **Five habit areas**, each self-scored 1–5: **Environment, Time, Appearance, Social, Financial.**
- **Rules:** your goals sit in the level just above where you scored; work on the lowest trait and the lowest habit area first; correcting weakness beats building strength.

## 5.3 Milgram obedience experiment

- Stanley Milgram (Yale, 1963): participants told by an experimenter to give increasingly strong "shocks" to a learner; a majority continued to the maximum level. Shows how readily ordinary people defer to perceived authority.
- Discussion angle: what made the authority believable (setting, role, calm insistence, shifting responsibility), and the responsibility that comes with building authority yourself.
- Cite: Milgram, "Behavioral Study of Obedience," 1963, https://doi.org/10.1037/h0040525.

## 5.4 Limiting beliefs

- Two ground rules: don't go on a witch hunt (let beliefs surface), and have a strategy for testing them.
- **Test questions:** Do I know an exception? Is this belief helping or limiting my life? Does it put me in charge? What would a good coach tell me?
- **Internal checklist themes:** not fully engaged in life, needing to be right, unresolved family issues, unclear values, attachments, living a lie, can't picture success, stress, social anxiety, neglecting health.
- **External prompts (complete the sentence):** "When I see someone confidently ask for money, I think they..."; "If someone has more status than me, I...".
- **Sample belief domains:** money, relationships, health, work, self.
- **Two exercises:** (1) mental — list beliefs, trace their source, find the opposite, talk to your past self as an objective friend; (2) emotional — revisit how the source event felt then versus now.

**Unit 5 practice:** complete the self-assessment and limiting-beliefs worksheet; write a dated milestone plan to raise the lowest trait and habit area by one level.
